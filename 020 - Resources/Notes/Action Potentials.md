# Definition
~
Sudden, all or nothing, uniform amplitude
Self perpetuating but signal can fade due to leakiness
Signal is passed to other neurons at terminals

# Action potential phases and ionic basis
*5 points*
~
1. Depolarisation 
	1. Slow depolarisation – stimulus/membrane creep 
	2. Rapid depolarisation – voltage gated Na+ channels
		1. Flood of positive ions into the cell
2. Overshoot 
	1. up to +30mV, voltage gated Na+ channels close 
3. Repolarisation 
	1. Voltage gated K+ channels open 
4. Hyperpolarisation 
	1. Slight ‘overshoot’ back to -70mV 
RMP – reestablished by Na+/K+ ATPase and K + leak channels
**Refractory period**
voltage gated Na+ channels transitioning 
Absolute refractory – cannot stimulate (during depolarisation) 
Relative refractory – need suprathreshold stimulus to trigger (repolarisation)

# Stimulus example
~
E.g. Capsaicin binding to C-type fibres opens gated ion channels (Ca2+) and triggers an action potential – heat activated channels (TRPV1).

# Action Potential Propagation
*Describe how an action potential is propagated*
~
- Will propagate itself but it is slow. 
- Excitation of adjacent segments – electrotonic current spread 
- Myelination = increasing conduction velocity 
	- Formed by Schwann cells 
	- Myelin sheath will insulate the nerve fibre AP signal jumps between ‘Nodes of Ranvier’ Signal regenerated at each NoR Myelin is key in nerve repair

# Clinical Relevance
*Describe the clinical relevance of myelination in a horse and dog*
~
Left recurrent laryngeal nerve is very long. If it is demyelinated, there will be muscular atrophy of the laryngeal cartilages and cause breathing problems

# Sensory Receptors and Nerve endings
*How do these factors impact action potentials?*
~
The intensity of stimulus is proportional to the frequency of action potentials -
- Stronger stimulus = more action potentials 
- More action potentials = greater activity at afferent terminals 
	- Signal transmission 
	- Neurotransmitter release
